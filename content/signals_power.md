## AA cells and lithium

Most handhelds, torches, and small radios eat AA cells. There are three kinds you will actually meet.

Alkaline. Cheap, in every shop. Fine above freezing for a torch and a radio you use in bursts. They sag when they are cold, and they leak if you leave them in a radio for a year. Take them out if the set will sit.

Lithium AA, the light metal cells (lithium iron disulphide), not a rechargeable lithium-ion pack. They cost more, weigh less, hold a voltage when alkaline has given up in the cold, and they store for years. Put these in the set you might need in January. They are not the same as the rechargeable packs that look like a battery block. Do not try to recharge a lithium AA. The wrapper says if it is single-use.

Rechargeable NiMH. The right everyday cell if you can charge them. They are empty more suddenly than alkaline: the radio works, then it does not. Carry a second set. A charger and a power bank are on the next cards.

Rules for any of them:

1. Do not mix old and new in one radio. Do not mix types. The weak cell gets forced and can leak or get hot.

2. Match the picture in the battery door. Backwards cells sometimes do nothing and sometimes damage the set.

3. Expiry dates matter for a set that sits in a bag. Rotate them. Use the oldest in the kitchen radio, put fresh ones in the grab bag.

4. A radio left on, even squelched, is a flat pack by morning. Off means off. Remove a cell if the switch is unreliable.

## Cold, heat, and spare sets

The survival manual's radio chapter is blunt about batteries. Cold steals the power. High heat can destroy a cell, and some types can fail violently if you cook them. Water ruins the set even when the case looks sealed.

1. Spare cells live in an inside pocket, against you, not in the rucksack lid overnight.

2. If the radio fades in the cold, swap in the warm spares. Put the faded set in the pocket. It may recover enough for one more call. Do not count on it.

3. Do not leave a radio or a power bank on a dashboard in the sun, or on a stove, or in a closed car in summer.

4. Dry the set before you open the battery door. Wet cells corrode the springs. If they have been soaked, bin the cells and dry the radio for a day before you try new ones.

5. Two sets of cells per radio is the minimum for a day out. Three if it is cold or you will listen for news as well as talk.

6. Test the spares when you pack. A sealed packet can still be flat if it is years old.

The listening plan saves more power than the brand of cell. Short calls, radio off between check-ins, one set on watch. That is the Radio procedure card.

## Solar, USB, and power banks

Charge by day. Spend it in short bursts. A panel the size of a paperback will not run a radio all evening in a British winter. It will refill a power bank if you give it sun and time.

1. Put the panel in full sun, tilted toward the sun, not behind glass if you can help it. A window works and is worse. Shade on one corner of some panels cuts the whole output. Watch the shadow.

2. Charge a power bank from the panel. Then charge the radio from the bank. A radio plugged straight into a tiny panel will brown out every time a cloud passes, and some radios do not like that.

3. USB: many handhelds and wind-up radios have a charge socket. Use the cable you have tested. Charge while you have mains, a car socket, or the bank, then unplug. A flat bank left plugged in can drain the radio back.

4. A power bank hates the cold as much as a cell does. Keep it in the pocket. Charge it before a trip. A bank that has sat for months may be asleep or empty. Wake it at home.

5. Do not charge a lithium pack on a bed, under a pillow, or unattended on a flammable pile. If a pack is swollen, hot, or smells sweet and sharp, stop using it. Get it to a recycling point. Do not puncture it.

6. A car USB socket is a charger only while the engine can spare it. Do not flatten the starter battery to fill a phone. Engine off, charger out.

7. Write on the bank, in tape, how many radio charges you actually got last time. That number, in that weather, is your plan. The number on the packet is the manufacturer's.

The News topic covers a wind-up radio, which is the set that still works when every cell is flat. Wind it, listen to the bulletin, and stop.
